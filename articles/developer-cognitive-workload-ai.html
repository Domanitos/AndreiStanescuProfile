The Shape of Developer Cognitive Workload in an AI World

Aug 31, 2026 · Andrei Stanescu · 6 min read

Over the past year my team went all-in on AI-assisted development — mostly Cursor-based workflows, plus a set of shared prompts and skills we maintain like any other internal tooling. The results have been real: we roughly doubled our PR rate and automated a lot of the recurring maintenance work that used to eat our sprints. But if you asked me what changed the most for the engineers themselves, it wouldn’t be how much work they do. It’s what kind of work their heads are doing.

The load didn’t shrink — it moved

There’s a comfortable story about AI that goes: the machine does the typing, the human does the thinking, so everything gets easier. What I actually observe is a redistribution. Writing code from scratch taxes recall, synthesis, and the slow, effortful building of a solution in working memory. Reviewing generated code taxes something different: sustained attention, evaluation, and holding a large surface area of “not written by me” code in your head at once. The first kind of fatigue feels like effort. The second kind feels like diligence — until it quietly stops happening.

Cognitive science has a useful distinction here between intrinsic load (the problem is genuinely hard), extraneous load (the representation makes it harder than it needs to be), and germane load (building the mental models that make future work easier). AI tools are fantastic at stripping away extraneous load — boilerplate, API syntax, the fifteenth unit-test variation. But they can silently convert germane load into passivity: if a model writes the design and you merely approve it, you never build the mental model that would let you debug it at 3 a.m.

You can’t review what you don’t understand

The single biggest risk I see with AI-heavy workflows isn’t bad code reaching production — quality gates catch most of that. It’s the erosion of ownership. A developer who ships five AI-generated components a day, each one reviewed at a glance, is accumulating approved code they don’t deeply understand. That debt doesn’t show up in DORA metrics. It shows up the first time production behaves unexpectedly and nobody in the room can explain why the system works the way it does.

So one of my rules for the team is simple: if you can’t explain it, you can’t merge it. Not “it looks right”, not “the tests passed” — you can walk me through why it does what it does. Verbalising the design is also, conveniently, the fastest way I know to rebuild the mental model the shortcut skipped.

Review fatigue is the new bottleneck

When PR rate doubles, review volume doubles with it, and reviewers hit the limits of their attention span. A 1,500-line AI-generated diff is cognitively a much worse artifact than three 500-line hand-written ones, because generated code carries no conversational memory of intent. We’ve adapted our working agreements around this: smaller diffs, acceptance criteria spelled out before generation starts, and a reviewer expectation that “looks plausible” is not an approving verdict. SonarQube and Snyk gates catch the mechanical issues; the humans are explicitly there for intent and design.

This is also where I push back on the “AI will let juniors do senior work” narrative. What AI lets you do is produce senior-shaped output. The senior part — the judgement about what should exist, what breaks at scale, what the next engineer will need — still has to come from somewhere, and right now it only comes from humans who have built the models in their heads.

Flow is still the scarcest resource

The most underrated cognitive effect of good AI tooling, in my experience, is positive: it protects flow. A huge share of developer mental energy used to leak out in micro-interruptions — checking docs, copying a pattern from Stack Overflow, context-switching to remember an API. Offloading that to an assistant keeps you inside the problem. My most productive engineers aren’t the ones who prompt the best; they’re the ones who use the assistant to stay in one continuous line of thinking for hours.

The catch: every tool that keeps you in flow also tempts you to thrash — regenerate, re-prompt, chase a slightly different answer. Watching for that thrash is now part of how I coach. If someone’s had the same file rewritten five times in an hour, the AI isn’t saving them cognitive load; it’s laundering it.

What we actually do about it

A few practices that have held up for us:

— Keep the author in the loop: generate in small chunks, read every chunk, and never paste wholesale.
— Treat prompting as externalised design: the prompt is the first draft of the solution, so it gets the same care a design doc would.
— Cap the diff, not the velocity: we’d rather have three clean PRs than one mega-diff that reviews itself into approval.
— Invest in understanding, not just output: study groups, deep-dives on incidents, and “explain it to me” moments in review are how we keep germane load alive.
— Measure the human stuff: engagement and sustainable pace tell you more about whether your AI workflow is healthy than raw PR counts ever will.

The takeaway

AI-assisted development didn’t make our jobs lighter — it made them different. The heavy lifting moved from producing code to evaluating it, from memorising syntax to protecting attention, from writing to owning. Teams that recognise that shift and actively manage it get the productivity without hollowing out their engineers’ understanding. Teams that don’t will discover, eventually and expensively, that the cognition they offloaded was the actual product all along.
