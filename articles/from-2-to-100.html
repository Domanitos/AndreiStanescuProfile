From 2 to 100: Bootstrapping an Engineering Department’s Culture Before It Has One

Sep 30, 2026 · Andrei Stanescu · 6 min read

When I joined Iris Software Group, I was the second engineer in a brand-new Romania department. Today that department has more than a hundred people. People ask what the growth plan was, and I usually disappoint them: there wasn’t a grand plan, there was a series of small decisions made when the team was tiny, which compounded in ways nobody could have predicted. That’s what this post is about, because the early months are the only time you get to build culture deliberately.

In month one, everything is precedent

A two-person department has no process to inherit and no history to lean on. That sounds like a weakness; it’s actually the most leverage you will ever have. How you review the first PR sets the expectation for the ten thousandth. How you handle the first missed deadline teaches everyone in the room what honesty about progress looks like. Culture in a new team isn’t what you write on a slide; it’s what you tolerate and what you celebrate when nobody’s watching yet. We tried to choose both on purpose.

Hire slowly at the start, because it compounds

The first ten hires define the next ninety. I’ve hired more than ten engineers end-to-end over the years, from sourcing to onboarding, and the single biggest difference between the early hires and later ones is what they could be judged against. When there is no team culture yet, you are betting on signals: ownership, curiosity, the instinct to make everyone around them better. Skills matter, but skills in a five-person team are table stakes. A mediocre-on-paper engineer who raises the team’s bar beats a brilliant one who lowers it, and at two people you feel that difference within a week.

Onboarding deserves the same respect. What someone experiences in their first week becomes their definition of normal. We over-invested there: a working environment from day one, a real task shipping in the first sprint, and a named person whose job was making sure the new engineer understood not just the code, but why we do things the way we do.

Rituals that earned their place

A few things we started early and never regretted: a demo of working software every two weeks, because celebrating shipped things beats celebrating plans; written code review standards, created when reviewing felt too slow to formalize and beloved later when volume made them essential; one-on-ones from the very beginning, even when the whole team fit in one conversation; and study groups, which started as a certification push and quietly became the strongest talent engine we have, driving twelve AWS and ISTQB certifications across the department and four promotions from within to Lead and Manager roles.

The ritual people are most surprised to hear me defend: we spent actual money on team events. When the department was ten people, an outing after a big release was just a dinner reservation; as we grew, we kept it deliberately structured so it wouldn’t quietly disappear the way unplanned culture does. Twice a year the whole department gets together, team-level outings happen quarterly, and the game nights (yes, I have opinions about which games a team of engineers should own you at) became the thing people mention first when you ask them what working here feels like. I don’t pretend a bowling evening builds culture by itself. What it does is give culture somewhere to happen: the conversation between two people who only knew each other as names in a review queue, the moment a new joiner stops feeling like a guest. Our engagement scores stayed at nine out of ten through a period of intense growth, and I would not claim the events caused that, but I am certain that dropping them would have been noticed.

What broke at scale (and how we noticed)

Here is the part growth posts usually skip: rituals that work at six people fail at forty. Everyone-in-everything meetings stopped scaling around twenty people, and the fix was structure, which itself was a culture problem: the first team leads I appointed felt like a betrayal of the flat, founding-team spirit. It wasn’t. Structure is what lets a culture survive its own success. The harder personal lesson was giving away the work that made the early days fun. When you’ve reviewed every line of code the department ever shipped, stepping back from that loop feels like losing something. You are: you’re trading it for a department that can grow without you in every loop, which is the only trade that scales.

Grow people faster than headcount

If I could keep only one outcome from those years, it wouldn’t be the headcount number. It’s that the people who grew this department also grew inside it. Four of my direct reports became Lead Engineers and Engineering Managers. Almost every practice that defines the team today was proposed, argued for, and owned by someone who joined when the whole thing fit in one room. A culture that grows its own leaders doesn’t need to keep re-founding itself every year.

The takeaway, if there is one: the culture of a hundred-person department is decided when it’s two people and nobody is watching. Choose your precedents on purpose, hire like the first ten are the next ninety, and build rituals while they still feel too small to matter. Scale doesn’t create culture. It just reveals, loudly, whatever you already had.
